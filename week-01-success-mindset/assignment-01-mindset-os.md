# Assignment 1. What is something you believe to be true that most people around you would disagree with?

### Rules

* No "safe" answers.
* Must be your real belief (not copied from internet).
* Minimum 50 words.

**Hint:** What do you believe about career, money, learning, discipline, relationships, health, success, life, tech industry, etc. that most people don't agree with?

## Answer

TI believe AI will replace a large part of traditional software development in the future. Many people around me believe that AI is simply a tool that will make developers faster, but I think it will go much further than that. As AI becomes better at writing, testing, debugging, and understanding code, companies may need fewer developers for work that is repetitive or follows well-known patterns.

I do not think this means developers will completely disappear. Instead, I believe the role of a developer will change significantly. People who only know how to write code may struggle, while developers who understand systems, architecture, business problems, DevOps, AI, and how to use AI effectively will become more valuable. This is one reason I think learning only a programming language is not enough for my career. I need to understand how technology works as a whole and learn how to work alongside AI rather than compete with it.

# Assignment 2. What are the top 3 objective truths you discovered through experimentation and results?

### Definition

Objective truths do not depend on opinions. They hold true regardless of how people feel.

Write each truth in this format:

## Truth #1

**Truth:** Practice exposes gaps in understanding that theory alone cannot reveal.

**Evidence from my life:**
While learning Python and SQL, I noticed that I could understand an explanation and still struggle when writing the code myself. Actually solving problems exposed mistakes that I did not notice while studying theory. Fixing those mistakes gave me a much clearer understanding of the concepts.

---

## Truth #2

**Truth:** Breaking a difficult technical problem into smaller concepts makes it easier to understand and solve.

**Evidence from my life:**
I have encountered technical topics in programming, DevOps, cloud, and AI that initially felt too complicated when viewed as a whole. When I started separating them into smaller concepts and learning them one at a time, they became much easier to understand. This showed me that complexity is often easier to handle when the problem is divided into smaller parts.

---

## Truth #3

**Truth:** Understanding a concept is more valuable when I can demonstrate it through practical work.

**Evidence from my life:**
While learning programming and SQL, I found that remembering definitions was not enough. Writing programs, creating queries, making mistakes, and correcting them showed whether I actually understood the concept. Practical application gave me stronger confidence than simply being able to explain the theory.

# My 2.0 Version

Three to seven years from now, I had built a career around understanding technology rather than only writing code. I had started with programming fundamentals and gradually developed strong skills in DevOps, cloud computing, automation, and AI-assisted development. Instead of being dependent on one programming language or one tool, I had learned how different parts of a modern technology system worked together.

I had worked in a professional technology role where I was responsible for building, deploying, and maintaining real applications and infrastructure. I had worked with Linux, Git, Docker, CI/CD pipelines, cloud platforms, networking, databases, and monitoring tools. I had also used AI as part of my development workflow for writing code, debugging, researching solutions, generating tests, and automating repetitive tasks. More importantly, I had learned to verify AI-generated solutions instead of blindly depending on them.

My GitHub had become a record of my practical learning. Instead of containing only small practice programs, it had included complete projects with proper documentation, deployment instructions, infrastructure configuration, CI/CD pipelines, and technical explanations. I had built and shipped projects that demonstrated my ability to solve real problems rather than simply complete tutorials.

I had also created a technical portfolio and published articles about things I had learned while working on projects. Writing about technical problems had improved my ability to explain complicated concepts clearly. My portfolio had shown both my technical skills and the progression of my career.

I had earned relevant cloud and DevOps certifications and, more importantly, had backed those certifications with practical projects and real implementation experience. I had contributed to open-source projects or technical communities where possible and had helped other learners understand concepts that I had already struggled with myself.

Professionally, I had progressed beyond being someone who only received instructions and completed assigned coding tasks. I had taken ownership of projects, participated in technical discussions, suggested improvements, and helped automate repetitive processes. I had developed the ability to look at a problem from the perspective of reliability, scalability, security, cost, and maintainability.

Financially, I had become more disciplined. I had tracked my income and expenses, built savings, and made career decisions based on long-term growth rather than only short-term salary. I had also understood that continuously improving my skills was one of the most important investments I could make.

The biggest difference between my current version and my 2.0 version was not that I had learned every technology. It was that I had developed the ability to learn new technologies quickly, solve unfamiliar problems, use AI effectively, and consistently turn what I learned into proof of work. I had become someone who could adapt as the technology industry changed instead of being afraid of the changes.

# Reflection – Week 1

## Biggest insight I got about myself this week

I realized that my biggest advantage will not be knowing one particular technology. Technology is changing quickly, especially with AI, so I need to develop the ability to learn, adapt, and apply new knowledge. I also realized that practical work gives me a better understanding than simply consuming information.

## My biggest weakness/loop I noticed

My biggest weakness is sometimes trying to understand too much at once. When a technical topic becomes complicated, I can spend too much time thinking about the entire subject instead of breaking it into smaller problems and working through them one by one. This can lead to overthinking and delayed action.

## One system I will implement from this week

I will have a focused learning session every weekday evening where I work on only one technical objective at a time. I will end each session by recording what I learned, what I completed, and what I need to work on next. This will help me turn learning into consistent progress and proof of work.
